Data · Horror Across Two Internets

Same films, different nightmares

We put Douban and IMDb scores side by side for 117 horror films. The averages agree almost perfectly. The individual films do not — and the disagreements follow a pattern.

WhatScares · August 2026 · Data snapshot from our 136-film catalog

Douban is China's dominant film platform — part IMDb, part Letterboxd, with rating counts that regularly reach the hundreds of thousands. Its scores almost never appear on English-language sites, which is a shame, because putting them next to IMDb's is like reading two reviews of the same haunted house written by people who walked through different doors.

Every film in the WhatScares catalog shows IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes and Douban scores side by side. 117 of our 136 films have both an IMDb and a Douban score, which is enough to ask a real question: do Chinese and Western audiences actually disagree about horror?

+0.09mean Douban−IMDb gap across all 117 films — in aggregate, near-perfect agreement
+0.52mean gap for Japanese, Korean and HK/Taiwan horror (29 films)
−0.20mean gap for 2020s releases (20 films) — recent hype doesn't travel

That first number is the methodological point: Douban voters are not systematically harsher or kinder than IMDb voters. Both platforms grade on a 0–10 scale, and across a hundred-plus films they land in the same place. Which makes the individual swings — up to 1.7 points in either direction — much more interesting. These aren't noise. They're taste.

Three clusters jump out. First, the Hong Kong canon: Mr. Vampire's hopping-corpse comedy, Stephen Chow's Out of the Dark, and Rigor Mortis — Juno Mak's 2013 elegy for exactly that tradition. These films were childhood television and VCD-rental staples for a generation of Chinese viewers. On Douban they're heritage; on IMDb they're footnotes.

Second, the James Wan school of haunted-house craft: Dead Silence (+1.7!), Insidious (+1.1), and The Conjuring (+0.6). Douban's horror crowd rewards mechanical scare-craft — setups that pay off, rules that hold — and doesn't dock points for being unfashionable. Dead Silence sits at 6.2 on IMDb, widely dismissed on release. Douban's 337,000 voters put it at 7.9.

Third, Korean genre cinema played straight: Train to Busan and the island revenge film Bedevilled, both at 8.6 — scores IMDb reserves for canonized classics. Story-forward, emotionally maximal horror does numbers on Douban.

The reverse list has its own logic. Hype doesn't cross the firewall. A Quiet Place and Nosferatu arrived in the West carried by enormous marketing moments; Douban voters met them cold, months later, as files on a hard drive, and graded the movie rather than the event.

Vibes-forward filmmaking pays a toll. Mandy's black-light doom, The House of the Devil's deliberate slowness, Possum's grey misery — films where atmosphere is the story get read on Douban as style without substance. Notably, this is not an "elevated horror" penalty across the board: Hereditary (7.3 → 7.4) and Midsommar (7.1 → 7.0) hold their scores almost exactly. Douban has no problem with slow and sad. It has a problem with slow and vague.

Canon status doesn't transfer. Halloween invented the modern slasher, and that invention is precisely what you can't feel in 2026 without the history attached: on Douban it's a 6.8 film about a man who walks. Even The Exorcist drops from 8.1 to 7.7. Meanwhile Psycho — 9.0, the highest Douban score in our catalog — shows the exemption clause: a perfect story survives any border crossing.

And Stree and Tumbbad are a reminder that voting blocs run both ways: IMDb's enormous Indian user base lifts Indian releases the way Douban's home crowd lifts Hong Kong ones. Every "global" score is somebody's home game.

Stephen Chow's Out of the Dark has been rated by more people on Douban than The Witch has on IMDb in some months — and by 88 times more people than have ever rated it on IMDb at all. This is a whole horror canon — jiangshi films, Taoist occult procedurals, Korean found-footage — that is mainstream in one hemisphere and functionally invisible in the other. If you've exhausted the Western catalog and want horror that a quarter-million people vouch for but nobody you know has seen, this table is a menu.

Methodology, honestly: our catalog is 136 hand-picked films, not a random sample — it skews toward films we think are worth watching, and 117 of them have scores on both platforms (Douban is missing several Western indies; IMDb barely registers some HK titles). Douban horror voters, like Letterboxd users, skew cinephile. Scores are a snapshot from August 2026, both on a 0–10 scale. We'd call the per-film gaps robust and the group means directional.

Why we track this

  • A second opinion you can't get elsewhere. When IMDb and Douban agree, you can trust the score. When they split, the gap itself tells you what kind of film it is — hype vehicle, craft object, or heritage piece.
  • The gems hide in the gaps. A film at IMDb 6.3 / Douban 8.0 isn't mediocre — it's mislabeled. That's how we surface hidden gems in the catalog.
  • Horror taste is cultural. Scare-craft travels; hype and canon status don't. Knowing which is which makes every score more useful.
